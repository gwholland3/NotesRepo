Parts of a Boat:

- Bow: front of the boat
- Stern: rear of the boat
- Port: left side of the boat
- Starboard: right side of the boat
- Hull: body of the boat
- Bilge: Interior of the hull below the floorboards
- Gunwale: upper edge of the boat’s side
- Cleat: metal fitting to which a rope can be fastened
- Keel: the bottom portion of the hull that increases stability in the water (often fin-like)
- Transom: vertical surface at the back of the hull
- Rudder: a vertical piece of metal that can be turned to steer the boat
- Outboard: consists of an engine, gear case, and propeller, attached to the transom
- Tiller: lever used to steer a rudder
  
  
  Boat Measurements:
- Beam: maximum width of the boat
- Freeboard: distance from the water to the lowest point on the boat where water could come on board
- Draft: depth of water needed to float the boat
- Overall length: measured from bow to stern, not including rudders or other extensions
	- Dictates what equipment the vessel is legally required to have
	  
	  
	  Types of Boat Hulls:
- Displacement:
	- Designed to push the water aside
	- Requires little propulsion
	- Limited to slower speeds
- Planing:
	- Designed to rise up and glide on the surface of the water when enough propulsion is applied
	- Can go at much higher speeds
	- As you increase speed with a planing hull, you’ll go from displacing (level, low in the water) to plowing (raised bow, low in the water) to planing (level, high in the water)
	  
	  
	  Boat Hull Shapes:
- Flat-bottomed:
	- Planing type
	- Shallow draft
	- Rides rougher in choppy waters
- Deep-Vee
	- Planing type
	- Smoother ride than flat-bottomed
	- Requires more power
- Round-bottomed:
	- Displacement type
	- Little propulsion required, even at slow speeds
	- Can roll easily without a good keel/stabilizer
- Multi-hull (think pontoon):
	- Displacement type
	- Greater stability
	- Large turn area/radius required
	  
	  
	  Types of Drives:
- Outboard drive
- Inboard drive
- Inboard/outboard drive
- Jet drive
- ### Capacity Plate
  A capacity plate is an information placard located on the boat that specified the maximum weight or number of passengers the boat can support without being liable to swamp or capsize.
- ### Float Plan
  A float plan is a common document that describes your intent to go out on the water. You can leave it with someone you trust or a local marina, to take action if something bad happens to you.
- ### Fueling
  Fueling a water vessel must be done with utmost care, since accidentally igniting fuel can be catastrophic.
  
  Before fueling:
- Have all passengers exit the boat
- Make sure the boat is tied down securely
- Make sure there is nothing that could cause a spark (engines, electrical equipment, matches, smoking, etc)
- Close as many boat openings (like doors and windows) as possible to prevent fuel fumes/vapor from entering the boat
- Have a fire extinguisher accessible
  
  While fueling:
- Keep the fuel nozzle stationary to avoid producing static sparks
- Don’t fuel your tank to the brim - leave room for fuel to expand
  
  After fueling:
- Run a power ventilation system if available, to eliminate fuel vapors from being present when the engine is started
  
  After you’ve refueled, the general rule of thumb for fuel use is:
- Use 1/3 tank to get out
- Use 1/3 tank to get back
- Keep 1/3 tank as reserve for emergencies
- ### Avoiding Collisions
  When two vessels have the chance to collide with each other, one usually becomes the give-way vessel and the other becomes the stand-on vessel:
- Give-way vessel: responsible for taking early and substantial action to keep away from the other vessel
- Stand-on vessel: maintains its course and speed, only taking action if the give-way vessel fails to do so
  
  A general rule of thumb is to turn starboard (right) to avoid a collision.
  
  
  Miscellaneous Terminology:
- Underway: a vessel that is not anchored, tied to shore, or aground
- Leeward: the direction in which the wind is blowing, or the vessel that is further downwind
- Windward: the direction that the wind is coming from, or the vessel that is further upwind
- Aground: touching or stuck on the ground
- ### Boat Lights
  There are common lights used to indicate the presence of one vessel to others:
- Sidelights: a red light on the port (left) side, and a green light on the starboard (right) side
- Sternlight: a white light on the vessel’s stern, visible from behind
- Masthead light: white light that shines forward and to the sides, indicates a powered vessel
- All-round white light: Combines the masthead light and the sternlight into a single white light that is visible from all directions
- ### Sound
  Common sounds used to convey information:
- One short blast: “I intend to pass you on my port side”
- Two short blasts: “I intend to pass you on my starboard side”
- Three short blasts: “I intend to use stern propulsion (back up / go in reverse)”
- One prolonged blast: indicates your presence
- Five or more short blasts: indicate danger or lack of understanding about another vessel’s intentions
- ### Buoys and Markers
  Green buoys mark the left side of a channel when you are heading away from open water, while red buoys mark the right side. Green buoys are shaped like cylindrical cans, while red buoys are shaped like cones. They are usually numbered, with red buoys using even numbers and green buoys using odd numbers. The numbers increase as you head away from open water.
  
  A junction buoy sits at the junction where one channel splits into two, and are both green *and* red. If the green is on top, the preferred/primary channel is to the right. If the red is on top, the preferred/primary channel is to the left.
  
  A safe water marker has red and white vertical stripes. It usually sits in the middle of a body of water, and indicates safe water on all sides.
  
  A mooring buoy is white with a blue horizontal stripe, and can be anchored/tied to by boats.
  
  An inland waters obstruction marker has white and black vertical stripes, and indicates that the water between it and the nearest shore has navigational obstructions.
  
  Other buoys or markers usually have text written on them to explain their purpose.
- ### Anchors
  Anchors are typically designed to grip the bottom when pulled horizontally, rather than vertically. A heavy chain between the anchor and the anchor line helps make most of the pulling force horizontal.
  
  Always anchor from the bow, never the stern. The stern is more susceptible to getting swamped by water, and the anchor’s weight doesn’t help.
