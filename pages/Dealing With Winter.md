The winter season can bring a harsher environment. These are some notes about how to prepare for and handle that harshness.

## <u>**CAR**</u>

## Ice
Often, ice will form on your front and back windshields overnight, and remain present by morning. This sucks, because the traditional way to get rid of it, so that you can see while driving, requires spending several minutes with an ice scraper.

Why does it form?

To put it simply: as temperatures drop, water molecules in the air ([water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor)) first [condense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation) onto your windshield, and then [freeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freezing) onto it. Let’s dive deeper into each of those steps.

### Phase 1
The sun is still up. There is water vapor in the air, but its temperature is high enough that it remains a vapor (read: gas) and doesn’t condense. Your windshield is also at a warm temperature.

### Phase 2
The sun goes down. Both the water vapor and the windshield begin to cool down, through the following means:
- The windshield is a pretty efficient radiator, so it [radiates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation) electromagnetic energy outward and into space, thus losing energy and reducing its own temperature. The fewer clouds or other covering mass there is, the quicker this radiation makes it to space, and the quicker the windshield’s energy is lost
- The water vapor and the other air molecules surrounding it do not radiate as efficiently, so this is a smaller component of their energy loss than it is for the windshield. However, since the air is in contact with so many other materials, many of which do lose a significant amount of energy via radiation (such as your windshield), it loses energy via 
- [kinetic interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conduction). The air is essentially passing its thermal energy to more solid objects to radiate away.
	- This means that the air is slightly coldest where it is in contact with colder, radiative objects

### Phase 3
As energy is radiated into space by all parties (and without the sun to restore that lost energy via its own radiation), water vapor eventually cools down enough that it reaches its dew point, at which it begins to condensate. Because the vapor is slightly colder next to solid objects, this is where it begins condensing first. This condensation begins to build up on your windshield.

### Phase 4
Your windshield’s temperature continues to drop via radiation, and it eventually reaches the freezing point of water. As it’s cooling, it is also cooling the condensed water accumulated on top of it via kinetic energy transfer. Once it hits the freezing point, that condensed water begins to freeze on top of the windshield.

Now you’ve got ice.


### How to Prevent/Remove

One decent source: [https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/how-do-i-get-ice-off-my-car-quick-tips-from-an-engineer-who-studies-airborne-particles](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/how-do-i-get-ice-off-my-car-quick-tips-from-an-engineer-who-studies-airborne-particles)

Removal is pretty simple. You’ve got two main options:
- Physically remove the ice: this can be done by a combination of scraping and windshield wiper movement
	- [Will this damage my windshield?](https://glasshelper.com/ice-scrapers-scratch-windshield/)Melt the ice first: apply heat to the ice such that it melts and can be wiped away as slush/water by your wipers. Heat usually comes from the car’s defrost system, which blows warm air at the inside of the windshield, warming it and through it the ice as well.

Prevention is more interesting. One option is to prevent your windshield temperature from dropping below the freezing point of water. If you insulate your windshield on the outside, either via parking under a cover (e.g. garage, covered parking spot) or manually covering it with something (like a dedicated car windshield cover), the rate of energy loss due to radiation will be reduced, and it might be able to retain enough heat through the course of the night to avoid going below the freezing point.

## Interior Condensation
This is similar to the formation of ice on the outside of your front windshield, except that you see water condensate on the *inside* of the windshield. This is also annoying, because it similarly blocks visibility.

An easy solution is to carry a towel that can quickly wipe the condensation off, but the downside to this is that you end up getting your windshield dirtier faster by smearing stuff around on it with the towel, and a dirty windshield generates condensation quicker than a clean one.

Given that the amount of condensation on the inside of your windshield is pretty much only dependent on the temperature and humidity of the interior air, there’s not much you can do for prevention. The air temperature, for instance, you can’t really control - it will get colder as the car does.

However, you can try to decrease the humidity slightly by two methods:
1. Opening your car windows: the assumption is that normally, the air inside the car is slightly more humid than the air outside, because you are breathing in it and introduce other sources of moisture. By opening the windows, you allow the humidity to decrease as it reaches an equilibrium with the outside air
2. Running the A/C: most cars’ A/C dehumidifies the interior air


## <u>**HOUSE**</u>

## Pipes Bursting
If water freezes inside a pipe, it will expand, and the pressure might be enough to crack the pipe. During winter, cold temperatures can be enough to cause water to freeze inside a houses pipes.

Usually, this happens because the water loses heat via an external faucet that is connected to its pipes. One cheap way to reduce the risk of water freezing is to purchase and install foam covers on your house’s external faucets, such as [this one](https://www.homedepot.com/p/Standard-Outdoor-Faucet-Cover-1980/204759226).

The insulation provided by the cover will reduce the rate at which heat is dispersed via the external faucet, so water temps won’t decrease so fast during the night.
