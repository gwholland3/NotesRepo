The molecules that make up soap are
- [Surfactants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfactant) ("surface-active agents"), which means they decrease the surface tension between two liquids (e.g. water and oil)
- [Amphiphiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphiphile), which means they are dipolar with one hydrophilic end and one lipophilic/hydrophobic end

One end of a soap molecule (the head) is hydrophilic - it is polar/charged and attracts water molecules.
The other end (the tail) is hydrophobic - it is nonpolar/neutral, repels water molecules, and attracts oil/fat/grease/dirt.

Soap is soluble in water, meaning the individual soap molecules will dissolve and spread apart in a mass of water, rather than remaining clumped together.

### How Does Soap Clean?

In the general case:
- Water dissolves the soap molecules
- Once past a certain concentration, individual soap molecules cluster together into [micelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micelle) (spherical structures with the heads pointed outward and the tails pointed inward)
- Those micelles tend to form around oil/grease fragments and separate them from each other and from other surfaces (e.g. skin, fabric, etc)
- Since the outer surface of the micelles is hydrophilic, rinsing water carries the micelles (and the trapped oil inside) away from the dirty surfaces
	- In other words, the micelles are soluble just like the individual soap molecules were, effectively making the oil fragments soluble as well

### Properties of Soap

Some soaps have different pH values. A higher pH can cause dryness…

"Syndet" (synthetic detergent) bars typically have a lower pH.

Some soaps have added moisturizers, for example:
- Glycerin (a humectant that draws in moisture)
- Ceramides
- Plant-based oils/butters like shea butter or olive oil
- Oatmeal
- Goat's milk
- Chamomile

The thing to be worried about: soap that strips natural oils without replacing them.

### Interaction With Skin

Human skin is covered in an "acid mantle" - a protective layer of oils and acids. It is protective because it promotes an acidic environment, which is unattractive to many pathogens. It also helps retain moisture.

If the pH of the acid mantle rises too high, the acid mantle is disrupted, which can lead to dryness or irritation. Transepidermal water loss (TEWL) is a part of dryness, I think.

So in summary, you want a bar soap with:
- Lower pH (ideally in the 5.0-5.5 range, though 5.5-7.0 is decent too), to avoid disrupting the pH of the skin's acid mantle
- Moisturizers, to replenish any lost oils during cleaning
	- However, many people will apply a dedicated moisturizer *after* cleansing, one that they leave on
