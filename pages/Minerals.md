Minerals are a type of micronutrient.

The key characteristic of a mineral is that in general, our bodies either cannot synthesize it on our own at all, or we cannot synthesize a sufficient quantity to meet our needs. So, we must acquire them through foods.

They are also inorganic (as opposed to vitamins, which are organic micronutrients).

## Calcium
Wikipedia: [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium)

Calcium is a chemical element with symbol Ca and atomic number 20. It is the most abundant mineral in the human body.

Its most well-known role in human nutrition is to provide structure, strength, and hardness to bones and teeth.

There is also calcium circulating in the blood that is critical for various signaling functions, such as:
- Muscle contraction
- Nerve transmission
- Blood clotting
Because of this, the body will pull calcium from bones to maintain blood calcium levels if calcium intake is not sufficient. The bones are treated as a "calcium reservoir" of sorts.

### Sources
Dairy products are some of the best-absorbed sources of calcium. This is because calcium absorption is boosted by Vitamin D, and dairy products are commonly fortified with Vitamin D.

For example:
- Milk
- Yogurt
- Cheese (especially hard cheeses like cheddar or parmesan)

Some canned fish contain soft edible bones that have highly available calcium.

There are also dark leafy greens with generous amounts of calcium:
- Collard greens
- Turnip greens
- Kale

Other miscellaneous sources include:
- Calcium-set tofu (look for "calcium sulfate" in the ingredients
- Almonds
- Sesame seeds or tahini

Some foods are fortified with calcium, such as:
- Breakfast cereal
- Orange juice
- Plant-based milks


## Iron
Wikipedia: [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron#Nutrition)

The main function of iron is to transport and store oxygen. It is a component of several proteins, such as hemoglobin and myoglobin, that interact with oxygen (this means those protein molecules literally contain iron atoms).

There are two main types of dietary iron: heme iron and non-heme iron. The former can be found only in animal-based foods, and it is highly bioavailable - about 15-35% is absorbed. The latter is found in nearly all iron dietary sources, and it is less bioavailable - less than 10% is typically absorbed.

Humans do not *need* heme iron to survive, but it is preferable due to its higher bioavailability.

### Sources

These sources specifically offer heme iron:
- Red meat (e.g. beef and lamb)
- Poultry
- Seafood
- Organ meats (e.g. liver)

These sources only offer non-heme iron:
- Beans
- Nuts and seeds (e.g. pumpkin seeds, cashews, sesame seeds)
- Leaf vegetables (e.g. spinach and kale)
- Tofu
- Fortified grains


## Potassium
Wikipedia: [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium#Nutrition)

Potassium's main role in the human body is to act as an electrolyte. It is the primary cation inside cells.

It helps generate the electrical gradient required for nerve impulses and muscle contraction, most notably the heartbeat.

It also may help preserve calcium in the body.

Too much or too little potassium can lead to electrolyte imbalance, causing symptoms such as:
- Abnormal heart rhythms
- Muscle weakness
- Nausea/vomiting

### Sources
- Potatoes (especially the skin)
- Spinach
- Bananas
- White beans
- Milk and yogurt
