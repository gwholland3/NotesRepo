## Asset Allocation / Diversification
See [this Vanguard article](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/articles/diversify-way-you-think-about-diversification.html).

Asset allocation is the way that you divide your investment funds across different asset classes.

The best thing you can do with your investments is diversify them **among assets that best meet your financial needs/goals at the current moment**.

Note, for example, that it is not smart to diversify by investing equally between stocks and bonds if you don't need investment stability for the near future, since you would be forgoing the higher yields that stocks will give you in the long run. Instead, you would want to invest mostly in stocks, **but diversify your stocks across multiple high-performing sectors and market caps**.

## Investment Types
According to [this Vanguard article](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/how-to-invest/asset-allocation), the three major asset classes are cash investments, bonds, and stocks (there are other classes, such as real estate and commodities, but they are less common).

<u>Cash Investments</u>
This refers to things like [savings accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savings_account), [treasury bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security#Treasury_bill), or [money market accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_market_account). Essentially, you're loaning your cash to people who promise a pretty reliable, fixed return rate.

Money market accounts generally yield a higher return rate than savings accounts (is what I hear).

Also, cash investments are generally the safest but lowest yield investments, compared to the other asset classes.

<u>Bonds</u>
See [Vanguard article](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/understanding-investment-types/what-is-a-bond) and [Robinhood article](https://robinhood.com/us/en/learn/articles/6D8ArYVa2bpoTYhdgxxYKh/what-is-a-bond/).

In terms of risk and returns, bonds are in between cash and stocks. Probably a pretty good option if you're looking for a bit of income and good stability.

<u>Stocks</u>
Better for growth. See [Vanguard article](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/understanding-investment-types/what-is-a-stock).

<u>Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) and Mutual Funds</u>
ETFs: see [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-traded_fund).
Mutual funds: see [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_fund).
Comparison: see [Vanguard](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/etfs/etf-vs-mutual-fund).

I'm grouping these together because they are so similar and often confused.

An ETF is basically a collection of investments all bundled up into a single package that you can invest in. Sometimes, but not always, there is a fee associated with owning shares of the ETF, to cover the work of whoever manages it.

A mutual fund is similar to an ETF, but is usually more actively managed and charges a higher fee. Think of it as a "higher class" version of an ETF.

## Glossary

<u>Market Capitalization</u>
See [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalization) and [Robinhood](https://robinhood.com/us/en/learn/articles/GByCbiOluG2kcWs1AF6m5/what-is-market-capitalization/).

Basically, it's a measure of the total value of a company, calculated as the number of shares times the share price.
Companies can be categorized as small-cap, mid-cap, or large-cap companies depending on their market capitalization.

<u>Implied Volatility</u>
See [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implied_volatility).

Most often represented as the expected percent change in the value of an asset a year from now.

<u>Risk Premium</u>
See [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_premium).

Risk premium is a measure of how much additional return an individual must expect on a risky asset (compared to a no-risk asset) in order for them to take on that extra risk.

Therefore, a risk premium is a quality of a given investor, and varies from investor to investor. A higher risk premium indicates a more risk-averse investor, and a lower risk premium indicates an investor who is more comfortable taking on risk.

<u>Expense Ratio</u>
See [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expense_ratio).

An expense ratio is the percentage of your investment that is collected to pay for management of that investment. For example, a 1% annual expense ratio means that every year, the value of your investment drops by 1%, with that value going toward the investment managers.
