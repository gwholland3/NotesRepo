### Terminology
- A "non-revolving" loan is essentially a one-time loan. Compare this to the money you are loaned through a credit card, which is ongoing/continuous
- A "single-draw" loan is one in which you receive the entire loan amount upfront, in one single draw
	- Compare to a "multi-draw" or "delayed-draw" loan, where you can receive portions of the loan at different times
- A "non-purpose" loan is one in which the borrower does not have to use the loan for a specific purpose
- A "full-recourse" loan is one in which the borrower is personally liable for the entire loan amount, meaning they can lose personal assets if they fail to repay the loan
	- Compare to a "non-recourse" loan, where the lender can only recover the collateral

### Collateral Call
A "collateral call" is when the value of your pledged collateral has decreased below some threshold in comparison to your loan amount, such that you either have to pledge additional collateral or make a payment on a portion of the outstanding loan balance.

### Maturity Date
Some (most? all?) loans have a "maturity date", which is the date on which the loan is expected to be paid back in full.

Some loans even include a penalty (e.g. a fee) if you pay off the full loan before the maturity date.

### Rates
The Secure Overnight Financing Rate ([SOFR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SOFR)) is an interest rate that is supposed to represent the lowest rate at which banks are lending money. In other words, a lender should feel entitled to charge an interest rate that is at least as high as the SOFR, because in theory, there is no one else charging lower than that for a borrower to turn to instead.

The SOFR is just one type of [overnight rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overnight_rate), a rate used by banks borrowing or lending money overnight (read: loans that are repaid in under 24 hours). Those types of loans take place in what is called the [overnight market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overnight_market), where banks loan money if they end the business day with surplus funds and borrow if they end the day with a fund deficit. It is because the loans are so short-term that their interest rates are the lowest.

The SOFR is often used as a basis for loan interest rates, with some amount of margin added on by the lender for guaranteed profit. More precisely, lenders often use a 30-day rolling average of the SOFR, to soften any potential volatility in the rate.
